01 — Why now

AI already writes the code. It still can't build the machine.

Software is getting very good at writing software. It is not getting better at spotting a problem worth solving, or at making a thing that works in the real world. The next decade belongs to people who can make code touch metal, motors and sensors. That can't be prompted. It has to be built, by hand, with mistakes.

02 — What you're really buying

Not a kit. The moment it works.

It does not begin with a robot. It begins with one part, one question, and a chance to see what happens. They connect a part, test it, change one thing, and watch the machine respond.

Moment 01

The first LED, in their pocket

No screens for the first weeks. They learn what current and resistance do by wiring things wrong and working out why. Most light an LED in the first session and take it home.

Moment 02

The first fault they fix alone

A loose wire, a motor backwards, code that surprises them. Real hardware has real faults. Finding one and fixing it is the day they stop asking you to sit with them.

Moment 03

The first machine that listens

A robot that does what they told it to. Not a video of one. One component becomes a response, a few responses become a build, and then the question changes: what else can I make it do?

09 — Before you decide

Is this right for your child?

We would rather have an empty seat than a kit that sits in a cupboard from week three. Honest answers below.

This suits your child if they

  • ✓Take things apart to see inside
  • ✓Get frustrated but come back to it
  • ✓Ask how things work more than why
  • ✓Are somewhere in the middle at school, not at the top
  • ✓Have never been given a real tool

Wait a year if your child

  • –Has board exams this year
  • –Needs a maths or science grade fixed right now
  • –Has never finished a long project and isn't ready to try

Does my child need any experience?

No. The first weeks have no screens, only circuits. They learn what the code controls before they write any. Most of our students had never wired a circuit before week one.

What age is Origin for?

Designed for ages 14 and up. Younger makers can build alongside an adult. There is no upper age: college students and hobbyists use it too.

Will my child be left stuck on a problem?

Real hardware has real faults. Your child gets a mentor to ask in doubt sessions and chat, so they stay with the cause and keep the project moving without you troubleshooting after work.

Is it safe?

Low voltages everywhere. The battery pack has its own protection circuit and charges over standard USB-C.

What do they have at the end?

Three working machines on the table, fifty-odd smaller builds behind them, the code they wrote, and a child who can tell you how each one works.
